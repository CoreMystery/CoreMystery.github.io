Project 1: Images of the Russian Empire

CS180: Introduction to Computer Vision and Computational Photography

This project reconstructs color photographs from glass-plate scans by aligning the green and red exposures to the blue reference channel. I implemented single-scale exhaustive search for low-resolution JPEGs and a coarse-to-fine image pyramid for full-resolution TIFF scans. I evaluated L2 and NCC for single-scale alignment and used raw-pixel NCC as the baseline metric for the final pyramid pipeline. As an optional Bells & Whistles extension, I used Sobel edge features to correct the difficult Emir image.

Overview and Channel Splitting

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii captured each scene through blue, green, and red filters. In each scanned plate, the channels are stacked vertically: blue at the top, green in the middle, and red at the bottom. I split the image into three equal-height bands, keep blue fixed as reference, and search for x and y translations that align green and red to blue.

JPEG inputs are 8-bit while full-resolution TIFF scans can be 16-bit, so I convert all channels to floating-point values on a common [0, 1] scale before alignment.

Top -> Blue Middle -> Green Bottom -> Red

Single-Scale Alignment on JPEGs

For low-resolution JPEG inputs, I use exhaustive search over displacements in [-15, 15] for both axes. I evaluated both L2 distance and NCC while scoring only an interior crop to reduce border artifacts.

$$ L_2(I_1, I_2) = \sqrt{\sum_p (I_1(p) - I_2(p))^2} $$ $$ \operatorname{NCC}(I_1, I_2) = \frac{(I_1 - \bar{I}_1) \cdot (I_2 - \bar{I}_2)}{\lVert I_1 - \bar{I}_1 \rVert_2\,\lVert I_2 - \bar{I}_2 \rVert_2} $$

Smaller $L_2$ is better, larger NCC is better.

Offset convention for all tables on this page is (x, y), where positive x shifts right and positive y shifts down.

Border Exclusion During Scoring

Borders in glass-plate scans often contain black margins, scratches, or mismatched framing that can bias the objective. I tested border exclusions of 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15%. The estimated offsets became stable for all three JPEG examples at 10%, and remained unchanged at 15%, so I selected 10% as the smallest stable scoring crop.

This 10% crop is applied only when computing alignment scores. After final shifts are found, I also remove only invalid overlap regions introduced by np.roll wraparound. I did not implement automatic plate-border detection and removal.

Multi-Scale Image Pyramid

Exhaustive search at full TIFF resolution is expensive and large shifts may exceed a small local window. I therefore align from coarse to fine using four downsampling stages, then refine around the upscaled estimate at each level.

1) Downsample by factor 2 repeatedly 2) Coarsest level search in ±15 3) Move to finer level 4) Double previous displacement estimate 5) Local refinement in ±2 6) Repeat to full resolution

Final pipeline settings are fixed across images: blue reference channel, 10% scoring crop, four pyramid stages, ±15 coarse search, and ±2 refinement search.

Bells & Whistles: Better Features - Emir of Bukhara

As an optional Bells & Whistles extension, I implemented edge-based alignment to address the raw-pixel NCC failure on Emir. Raw-pixel NCC performs well on most examples but fails for Emir due to strong cross-channel brightness differences in the clothing and scene content. The green channel aligns correctly at (24, 49), but raw NCC places the red channel at (-205, 141), causing severe color ghosting.

I addressed this by computing Sobel edge magnitude and running the same pyramid NCC alignment on edge features instead of raw intensities. This emphasizes geometric structure over absolute color brightness. Sobel edges are used only to estimate channel displacements, the resulting offsets are then applied to the original red and green intensity channels to reconstruct the final RGB image.

$$ M = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2} $$

Here, $G_x$ and $G_y$ are the horizontal and vertical Sobel gradients, and $M$ is the edge-magnitude image used for matching.

Edge-based matching is more robust for Emir because it aligns structural boundaries instead of raw cross-channel brightness.

Limitations

Most images align cleanly after translation, but some still show colored bands near plate boundaries because original exposure borders are not identical. I did not implement automatic border detection and removal, since it is an optional extension.

Some residual local fringing can also remain around moving subjects because the three filtered exposures were captured sequentially. A single global translation cannot perfectly correct object motion between exposures.

For example, small color fringes around some people in Harvesters may reflect movement between exposures rather than an incorrect global channel shift.

Conclusion

This project shows that a simple translation-based alignment can reconstruct convincing color from Prokudin-Gorskii plates. Exhaustive search is effective for small JPEGs, while a coarse-to-fine pyramid makes the method practical for large TIFF scans. Border exclusion improves metric stability, and fixed pyramid parameters generalize across the provided dataset and custom examples. The key failure case was Emir, where raw cross-channel intensity matching breaks down, replacing raw intensity with Sobel edge magnitude corrected the alignment without changing the core search procedure.
